# Teaching *Things Fall Apart*: A Four-Week Unit for Year 11

This is the unit our department has taught for three years, revised after each run. It assumes students have read the novel over the half-term break, which in practice means about two thirds of them have, so week one is built to work either way. Page references are to the Penguin Modern Classics edition; quotations are kept short in the resources so that students go back to the text rather than relying on our extracts.

## Why this novel, and what we want students to do with it

Achebe wrote *Things Fall Apart* partly in answer to European novels that described Africa as a backdrop with no history of its own. Students don't need that context to enjoy the book, but they need it to understand its *form*: the first two thirds are a detailed, unhurried portrait of Umuofia as a complete society, with its own law, religion, economy, arguments and jokes, before the missionaries and the colonial administration arrive. The pacing is the argument.

By the end of the unit, students should be able to write a sustained analytical response on one of three big questions:

1. How does Achebe present Okonkwo as both a product of his society and at odds with it?
2. How does the structure of the novel shape the reader's response to colonisation?
3. What is the role of language — proverbs, stories, translation — in the novel?

## Week 1: Umuofia before the missionaries

**Lesson 1. Opening chapter.** Read chapter 1 aloud. Ask students to list everything we learn about Okonkwo and everything we learn about Unoka, his father, then to look at how much of each list is *defined against the other*. This sets up the central idea that Okonkwo's whole personality is a reaction to his fear of resembling his father.

**Lesson 2. Proverbs.** The narrator observes that among the Igbo the art of conversation is held in high regard, and proverbs are how it is carried. Give groups six proverbs from the first five chapters and ask what each one does *in its scene*: persuade, soften a refusal, show respect, display status. Students usually start by translating proverbs into morals; the goal is to get them talking about proverbs as actions.

**Lesson 3. Community and law.** The trial scene with the *egwugwu* in chapter 10. Discuss what kind of justice this is, how disputes are resolved, and who gets to speak. Students who arrive thinking pre-colonial societies had no institutions usually leave this lesson unsettled, which is the point.

## Week 2: Okonkwo's fall

**Lesson 4. Ikemefuna.** The most discussed chapter, and the one most students remember. Handle with care: some will find it upsetting. Focus the analysis on why Okonkwo takes part despite being advised not to, and on how the narration slows down and then refuses to linger.

**Lesson 5. The accident and exile.** Okonkwo's gun explodes at a funeral and kills a boy, and he is exiled to his mother's village for seven years. Discuss the idea that his punishment is not for malice but for an offence against the earth goddess. What does that tell us about how this society understands crime?

**Lesson 6. Mbanta.** In exile, Okonkwo is told that "mother is supreme". Contrast with his contempt for anything he considers feminine. Students can track this theme back through weeks one and two.

## Week 3: The arrival of the missionaries

**Lesson 7. Structure.** Show students a simple bar chart of how many chapters are spent on each part of the novel. Part One is far longer than Parts Two and Three combined. Ask: what is the effect of spending so long on Umuofia before the change comes? Most students will say it makes the reader care about what is lost; push for more, such as how it makes the colonial view of the Igbo as people "without culture" look plainly false.

**Lesson 8. Mr Brown and Mr Smith.** Two missionaries, two approaches. Compare the scenes in which each appears, and ask which is more dangerous to Umuofia and why. There is a good argument for each answer.

**Lesson 9. Nwoye.** Okonkwo's son converts. Students should connect this back to Ikemefuna and to Okonkwo's treatment of Nwoye throughout.

## Week 4: The ending and the essay

**Lesson 10. The final chapter.** Read the last two pages together, slowly. The perspective shifts to the District Commissioner, who is planning a book and thinks Okonkwo's story might make an interesting paragraph. Ask what the novel we have just read is doing that the Commissioner's book would not. This is the moment the whole unit is built towards.

**Lesson 11. Planning.** Students choose one of the three questions and build a plan around four or five moments in the text, each with a short quotation and a sentence of analysis. Model one paragraph live on the board, including a bad first attempt.

**Lesson 12. Timed write.** Fifty minutes, no notes beyond the text. Mark against the department rubric.

## Assessment and common weaknesses

Across three years the same weaknesses appear:

- **Retelling instead of analysing.** The fix is the plan in lesson 11: if a paragraph's sentence of analysis could be deleted without losing anything, it isn't analysis.
- **Treating Okonkwo as simply a villain or a hero.** The strongest essays hold both readings at once.
- **Ignoring structure.** Very few students mention the final chapter's change of perspective unprompted, and those who do almost always reach the top band.
