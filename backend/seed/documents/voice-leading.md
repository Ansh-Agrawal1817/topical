# Voice Leading in Four-Part Harmony

A handout for my Grade 6–8 theory students, and for anyone who has been told "you've got parallel fifths" without being told why anyone cares. The rules below are the conventions of eighteenth-century chorale style. They are not laws of music. They describe what makes four independent voices *sound* independent, and once you know why a rule exists, you'll know when a composer is breaking it on purpose.

## The four voices

| Voice | Usual range | Clef |
|---|---|---|
| Soprano | C4 – G5 | Treble |
| Alto | G3 – C5 | Treble |
| Tenor | C3 – G4 | Bass (or treble, sounding an octave lower) |
| Bass | E2 – C4 | Bass |

Keep adjacent upper voices within an octave of each other: soprano and alto, alto and tenor. Tenor and bass may be further apart, up to a twelfth. Wide gaps in the upper voices make the chord sound hollow, as if it were two separate groups of singers.

## The one idea behind most of the rules

Each voice should sound like a singable melody in its own right, and the four should stay audibly separate. Almost every rule follows from that.

- **Prefer steps to leaps.** A voice that moves by step is easy to sing and easy to follow. The inner voices especially should move as little as possible, often just holding a common tone.
- **Don't cross voices.** If the alto goes above the soprano, the listener's ear follows the higher note and loses track of which line is which.
- **Avoid parallel perfect fifths and octaves.** When two voices move in parallel by a perfect fifth or octave, they fuse into one sound, and you briefly have three voices instead of four. That is why the rule singles out *perfect* intervals: parallel thirds and sixths sound sweet precisely because the two voices remain distinct.

## Doubling

A triad has three notes and we have four voices, so one note is doubled.

- **Root position:** double the root. It is the most stable choice.
- **First inversion:** double whichever note gives the smoothest voice leading. The soprano note is often a good choice; avoid doubling the leading note.
- **Never double the leading note** (the seventh degree of the scale, B in C major). It has such a strong pull to the tonic that doubling it either produces parallel octaves when both resolve, or forces one of them to go somewhere unnatural.

## Resolving tendency tones

Two notes in a dominant seventh chord want to move in particular directions:

1. The **leading note** rises by a semitone to the tonic.
2. The **seventh** falls by step.

In V7 to I in C major, B goes up to C and F goes down to E. If you ignore these, the progression still has the right chords but sounds oddly unresolved, as though the music changed its mind.

The one common exception: when the leading note is in an *inner* voice, it may fall to the fifth of the tonic chord, so that the tonic chord can be complete. Bach does this constantly, and most examiners accept it in an inner voice.

## A worked progression

Let's harmonise I–IV–V–I in G major, with the soprano line B–C–A–G.

1. **I (G–B–D):** soprano B. Put the bass on G and double it, with alto D and tenor G.
2. **IV (C–E–G):** soprano C. The tenor holds its G, which is a common tone. Alto moves D–E by step, and the bass falls G–C.
3. **V (D–F♯–A):** soprano A. Watch the bass move C–D: if the soprano also moved by a fifth or octave in the same direction, we would have parallels. Here the soprano falls C–A while the bass rises, which is contrary motion and safe. Alto E–F♯, tenor G–A, both by step.
4. **I (G–B–D):** soprano G. The leading note F♯ in the alto rises to G, and the bass falls D–G. The tenor is on A, and the tempting move is down a step to G. Do that and the final chord is G–G–G–G: four voices and no third. Move the tenor *up* a step to B instead. The chord is G–G–B–G, with no fifth, which is allowed, and a third, which is not optional.

That last chord is exactly where students go wrong. The habit to build is checking every chord for its third before you check anything else.

## Checking your work

Go through every pair of voices, all six pairs, and look at each chord change:

- Is there a parallel fifth or octave? Look for it between the outer voices first, then between soprano and tenor, and so on.
- Does any voice leap by more than an octave, or by an augmented interval?
- Is the leading note doubled anywhere?
- Do the seventh and the leading note resolve?
- Is every chord complete? It is acceptable to omit the fifth of a root-position chord, but never the third.

It takes a long time at first. After a few weeks you will start *hearing* the parallels before you see them, and that is when this stops being a list of rules and becomes a way of listening.
