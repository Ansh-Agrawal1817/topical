# Phonemes, Allophones and Minimal Pairs

Notes for the second-year phonology seminar. The concepts here are simple to state and surprisingly slippery to apply, mainly because native speakers are very bad at hearing differences their language doesn't use. Most of the exercises are designed to make you hear those differences.

## Sounds versus categories

Say *top* and *stop* with your hand in front of your mouth. In *top* you should feel a puff of air after the *t*; in *stop*, almost none. Phoneticians write the first as [tʰ], an aspirated t, and the second as [t].

Most English speakers have never noticed this. To them both are simply "t". That is the central idea of phonology: a language groups many physically different sounds into a smaller set of categories that its speakers treat as "the same sound". Those categories are **phonemes**, written between slashes: /t/. The different physical realisations of one phoneme are its **allophones**, written in square brackets: [tʰ], [t], and in many accents [ʔ] in *button* or [ɾ] in American *water*.

The same pair of sounds can be one phoneme in one language and two in another. In Hindi, [t] and [tʰ] distinguish words: *tal* "beat" and *thal* "plate" mean different things. A Hindi speaker hears the difference immediately. For an English speaker it takes training.

## Minimal pairs

The standard test for whether two sounds are separate phonemes in a language is to find a **minimal pair**: two words that differ in just that one sound and mean different things.

| Pair | Sounds | Conclusion for English |
|---|---|---|
| *pat* / *bat* | [p] / [b] | separate phonemes /p/, /b/ |
| *ship* / *sip* | [ʃ] / [s] | separate phonemes /ʃ/, /s/ |
| *thigh* / *thy* | [θ] / [ð] | separate phonemes, though very few minimal pairs exist |
| *lip* / *pill* | clear [l] / dark [ɫ] | no minimal pair: allophones of /l/ |

That last row is worth a minute. English has a "clear" l at the start of a syllable and a "dark", velarised l at the end of one. Swap them and the word sounds foreign or odd, but it doesn't turn into a different word. So they are allophones.

The absence of a minimal pair is weaker evidence than its presence. Some genuinely separate phonemes have very few minimal pairs because of accidents of vocabulary, as with /θ/ and /ð/ in English.

## Complementary distribution

Allophones usually occur in predictable, non-overlapping environments. This is called **complementary distribution**. English aspiration works like this:

- [tʰ] at the start of a stressed syllable: *top*, *attack*;
- [t] after /s/ in the same syllable: *stop*, *star*;
- [t] or [ʔ] at the end of a word in many accents: *cat*.

Because the environment predicts which allophone appears, we can write a rule rather than listing every case:

$$/t/ \rightarrow [t^{h}] \;/\; \_\_\,\acute{V}$$

read as "/t/ becomes aspirated before a stressed vowel at the start of a syllable". (The full rule needs to mention syllable position, which this shorthand glosses over.)

Two sounds in complementary distribution are *candidates* for allophones of one phoneme, not proof. The classic counterexample is English [h] and [ŋ]: [h] occurs only at the start of syllables and [ŋ] only at the end, so they never contrast, but no linguist treats them as one phoneme because they share almost no phonetic features. Phonetic similarity is part of the argument.

## Free variation

Sometimes two sounds can occur in the same position without changing meaning: many speakers say *economics* with either /iː/ or /ɛ/ in the first syllable. This is **free variation**. It is less free than the name suggests; the choice often correlates with region, age or formality, which is where phonology hands over to sociolinguistics.

## Exercises

1. Find minimal pairs to show that /v/ and /w/ are separate phonemes in English. Then explain why speakers of some languages find this contrast hard to hear.
2. In Spanish, [d] and [ð] are in complementary distribution: [d] after a pause or a nasal, [ð] between vowels (*dedo*, "finger", is [ˈdeðo]). Write the rule. Are they one phoneme or two? What evidence would change your mind?
3. Record yourself saying *latter* and *ladder* in a natural sentence. Are they the same? If they are, what does that tell you about your accent's rule for /t/ between vowels?
4. Korean has three contrasting stops at each place of articulation: plain, aspirated and tense. Why would an English speaker probably hear two of them as "the same"?
