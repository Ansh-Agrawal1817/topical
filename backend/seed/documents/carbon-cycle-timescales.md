# The Carbon Cycle on Three Timescales

Most diagrams of the carbon cycle show arrows between boxes, and almost none of them say how *fast* each arrow is. That omission is the source of a lot of confusion, including the common question "if forests absorb carbon, why can't we just plant more trees?". The carbon cycle is really several cycles running at very different speeds, and which one matters depends on the question you are asking.

These notes were written for an outreach session with sixth-formers, and later expanded for our MSc study group.

## The reservoirs

Approximate stores of carbon, in petagrams (1 Pg = 1 billion tonnes):

| Reservoir | Carbon (Pg C) |
|---|---|
| Atmosphere | ~870 |
| Land vegetation | ~450 |
| Soils and permafrost | ~3,000+ |
| Surface ocean | ~900 |
| Deep ocean | ~37,000 |
| Fossil fuel reserves | ~1,000–2,000 |
| Sedimentary rocks | ~60,000,000+ |

The atmosphere is one of the *smallest* reservoirs. That is why moving even a small fraction of the larger stores into it changes the climate.

## Fast: years to decades

**Photosynthesis and respiration.** Land plants take up roughly 120 Pg C per year and return almost exactly as much through respiration and decay. The ocean surface exchanges a similar amount with the atmosphere. These fluxes are enormous compared with human emissions of about 10 Pg C per year, but they are nearly balanced, so they move carbon *around* rather than removing it.

You can see this cycle directly. The famous Mauna Loa record of atmospheric CO₂ has a sawtooth on top of its rising trend: it falls every Northern Hemisphere summer as forests grow, and rises every winter as leaves decay. That annual swing is about 6 ppm, the planet breathing.

The catch with trees as a climate solution is that carbon stored in a forest is only stored for as long as the forest stands. A fire, a pest outbreak or a change in land use returns it to the atmosphere within years. Forests matter enormously, but they are a fast store, not a permanent one.

## Medium: centuries to millennia

**The ocean.** The surface ocean absorbs CO₂ quickly, within about a year it comes close to balance with the air above it. But the surface is a thin layer. Carbon reaches the deep ocean slowly, carried down by sinking cold water at high latitudes and by the "biological pump" of dead plankton sinking out of the surface. The deep ocean turns over on a timescale of around a thousand years.

So of the CO₂ we emit, a large share is taken up by the ocean over decades to centuries, but the ocean's ability to absorb more depends on how fast the surface can pass it down. As the ocean takes up CO₂ it also becomes more acidic, which reduces its capacity to absorb further CO₂ over time.

A useful rough summary of what happens to a pulse of emitted CO₂:

- about half is removed from the atmosphere within a few decades, by land and ocean together;
- most of the rest is removed over several centuries as the ocean mixes;
- around 20% remains in the atmosphere for many thousands of years.

That last fraction is the one people find surprising, and it is why "net zero" is about stopping emissions rather than reducing them.

## Slow: hundreds of thousands to millions of years

**Weathering and volcanism.** On geological timescales the dominant processes are chemical. Rain is slightly acidic because it dissolves CO₂, and it slowly weathers silicate rocks. The products wash into the ocean, where organisms build carbonate shells that eventually settle as limestone. Over millions of years, plate tectonics carries some of that carbon into the mantle and volcanoes return it as CO₂.

This cycle is the planet's thermostat. Warmer, wetter climates weather rock faster, which removes CO₂ and cools the climate; colder climates weather less, letting volcanic CO₂ build up. It has kept Earth habitable for billions of years. It is also far too slow to help us: it operates on timescales of hundreds of thousands of years.

## Putting the timescales together

The fossil fuels we burn were drawn out of the fast cycle over millions of years and locked into the slow one. Burning them moves that carbon back into the fast cycle within a couple of centuries. The fast and medium cycles redistribute it among the atmosphere, land and ocean, but only the slow cycle removes it for good.

That is the whole problem in one sentence: **we are moving carbon from the slow cycle to the fast one about a hundred times faster than the slow cycle can move it back.**

## Questions for discussion

1. If land vegetation absorbs about 120 Pg C per year, why is it wrong to say it "cancels out" our 10 Pg C of emissions?
2. Why does the seasonal swing in CO₂ measured in Antarctica look much smaller than the one at Mauna Loa?
3. Some proposals for removing CO₂ involve spreading crushed silicate rock on farmland. Which of the three cycles is that trying to speed up, and what would you want to know before believing it works?
