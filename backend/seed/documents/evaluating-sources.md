# Evaluating Sources: A Workshop for First-Years

A ninety-minute session we run in the first month of term for undergraduates in every faculty. It replaces an older version built around checklists, which students completed dutifully and then ignored. The current version is built around one habit borrowed from professional fact-checkers: **leave the page to find out about the page**.

## Why checklists failed

The old workshop used a checklist asking about currency, relevance, authority, accuracy and purpose. It looks thorough. In practice students applied it by staying on the source and judging it by how it looked: a professional design, an "About us" page, citations at the bottom, a .org address. Every one of those signals can be faked cheaply, and the most misleading sources are usually the ones that have bothered to fake them.

Researchers at Stanford compared how historians, students and professional fact-checkers judged unfamiliar websites. The fact-checkers were faster and far more accurate, and the difference was mostly one behaviour: within seconds of landing on an unfamiliar site, they opened new tabs and looked up what *other* sources said about it. This is called **lateral reading**, and it is what the workshop teaches.

## The session plan

### 1. Warm-up: two websites (15 minutes)

Students are shown two websites about the same health topic. One belongs to a long-established professional body; the other to an advocacy group funded by an industry with an interest in the topic. Both look polished. Both cite research.

Pairs have five minutes to decide which they would trust in an essay, and why. Most choose based on design, tone or the presence of references. Roughly half choose the advocacy site.

We don't reveal the answer yet.

### 2. Lateral reading (25 minutes)

Demonstrate on the projector, thinking aloud:

1. Open a new tab. Search for the organisation's *name*, not the topic.
2. Read what others say about it: news coverage, Wikipedia (as a map, not a citation), funding disclosures, government registers of charities or companies.
3. Ask: who is behind this, what do they want, and what do independent sources say about their reliability?

Then let the pairs redo the warm-up with this method. It usually takes under two minutes per site, and the class gets the right answer almost unanimously. The point to dwell on is not that the advocacy group is "bad". It is that *the site itself was never going to tell you* who funded it.

### 3. Click restraint (10 minutes)

The second habit: don't trust the first search result. Scan the whole first page of results, look at the sources' names and descriptions, and choose deliberately. Search engines rank by relevance and popularity, not reliability.

### 4. Finding the original (20 minutes)

Students are given a news article that reports a striking statistic from "a new study". Their task is to find the study, read its abstract, and check whether the article reported it accurately.

What they typically find, depending on the example we use:

- the study was in mice, and the article doesn't say so;
- the effect was real but small, and the headline reported the relative risk instead of the absolute one;
- the "study" was a press release or a survey commissioned by a company.

This exercise is where students start getting genuinely annoyed with the media, which is a good sign. Steer that energy into the habit: *for anything you are going to rely on, find the original.*

### 5. What counts as a source in your discipline (15 minutes)

Split by faculty. Each group gets a one-page sheet from their subject librarian about the main databases and the kinds of source their tutors will expect: peer-reviewed articles in most sciences, primary documents in history, case law in law, and so on. This is also where we explain why "it's on Google Scholar" doesn't mean "it's peer-reviewed".

### 6. Wrap-up (5 minutes)

Three questions to ask of any source:

1. **Who is behind it?** Find out by leaving the page.
2. **What is the evidence?** Find the original if it matters.
3. **What do other sources say?** Look for agreement among independent sources, not just one confident one.

## Notes for librarians running it

- **Change the examples every year.** Students share them, and old examples stop working as the websites change.
- **Avoid political examples for the warm-up.** Students defend the site they agree with, and the session becomes an argument. Health and consumer topics work better.
- **Don't present lateral reading as a trick.** It is how careful readers have always worked, with better tools.

Feedback from the last two years has been the best we have had for any library session, mostly because students said it was the first time someone showed them *how* to check something, rather than telling them that they should.
