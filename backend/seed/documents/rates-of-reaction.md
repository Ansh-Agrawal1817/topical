# Rates of Reaction — Year 10 Unit Plan

Six lessons, about fifty minutes each, plus one required practical. Written for a mixed-attainment set; the stretch questions at the end of each lesson are where the top third live. Everything here has been taught at least twice, and the timings are what actually happens, not what the scheme of work pretends.

## What students should leave with

By the end of the unit a student should be able to:

- explain, using collision theory, why concentration, pressure, surface area, temperature and catalysts change the rate of a reaction;
- calculate a mean rate from a table or a graph, with units, and a rate at a point by drawing a tangent;
- describe the required practical (the effect of concentration on the rate of the thiosulfate–acid reaction) well enough that someone who missed the lesson could carry it out;
- spot the three classic misconceptions below in their own writing.

The last one matters more than it sounds. Most marks lost on this topic in the summer exam are lost to a single sentence: "the particles collide more". That sentence is true and earns nothing.

## The misconceptions to hunt down

**"More collisions" instead of "more frequent collisions".** Rate is about collisions *per second*. A beaker of dilute acid left for a week will have an enormous number of collisions in total. Insist on "more frequent" or "more collisions per second" from lesson one, and be boring about it.

**Temperature only makes particles move faster.** It does, but the bigger effect is that a larger fraction of collisions now have at least the activation energy. A 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate for many reactions; the speed of the particles goes up by only a few per cent. Students find this genuinely surprising, which is a good sign.

**Catalysts "give energy" to the reaction.** A catalyst provides a different route with a lower activation energy. It is not used up and it does not change the energy of the products. The energy profile diagram in lesson 4 is where this gets fixed.

### Lesson 1: measuring rate

Start with two reactions side by side: magnesium ribbon in 1 M hydrochloric acid, and a marble chip in the same acid. Ask which is "faster" and how they would *prove* it. Most groups suggest timing until the fizzing stops, which is a perfectly good first idea and leads naturally to the question of what to measure *during* the reaction.

The main practical is calcium carbonate with hydrochloric acid on a balance, reading the mass every thirty seconds for five minutes. Use a cotton wool plug in the neck of the flask; without it, acid spray lands on the balance and the technicians will not forgive you.

The mean rate over an interval is

$$\text{mean rate} = \frac{\text{change in mass}}{\text{time taken}}$$

with units of g/s. Make students write the units every time. The higher tier also needs rate in mol/s, which is easier to introduce in lesson 5 once they are comfortable with the graph.

### Lesson 3: the required practical

The disappearing cross. Sodium thiosulfate solution reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to form a fine precipitate of sulfur, which slowly hides a cross drawn on paper beneath the flask.

Practical points that save the lesson:

- Make the thiosulfate dilutions yourself the day before, labelled 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 g/dm³. Students diluting their own wastes twenty minutes.
- Use the *same* piece of paper and the *same* person looking down for every run. The end point is a judgement, and changing observers is the biggest source of scatter.
- Sulfur dioxide is produced. Run it in a well-ventilated room, keep asthmatic students back from the flasks, and pour the mixtures into a sodium carbonate solution afterwards rather than down the sink.

Students then plot 1/time against concentration. The near-straight line is the payoff, and it is worth five minutes of discussion about *why* 1/time is a measure of rate at all.

### Lesson 4: temperature and catalysts

Hydrogen peroxide decomposing is a good catalyst demonstration because the difference is so dramatic: almost nothing with a sand blank, steady bubbling with a pinch of manganese(IV) oxide, and a foam over the top with a cube of liver. Potato is the good middle case.

Then the energy profile diagram. Draw the uncatalysed hump first, then the catalysed route as a lower hump starting and finishing at the same levels. The question to ask is: "What has the catalyst changed, and what has it left alone?" If they say "it made the products lower", the misconception is still there.

## Assessment

The mini-test in lesson 6 is ten questions, three of them six-markers. Mark them before the next lesson and re-teach only what more than a third of the class got wrong. In practice this is almost always one of two things: explaining the temperature effect fully, or reading a gradient off a curved graph.

### Stretch questions

1. A student repeats the thiosulfate experiment at 40 °C instead of room temperature. Predict and explain how the graph of 1/time against concentration will change.
2. Powdered calcium carbonate reacts with acid faster than chips, but the total volume of gas produced is the same. Explain both parts of that sentence.
3. Why might a catalyst that works well in the lab be useless in an industrial reactor? (There is no single right answer. Good answers mention poisoning, surface area, and cost.)
