# Renal Handling of Sodium

Revision notes from my renal block, cleaned up for the study group. The aim is to be able to answer "where is sodium reabsorbed, how, and what changes it" for any segment of the nephron, because that one question explains most of the diuretics and half of the electrolyte questions in the exam.

## The numbers to keep in your head

The kidneys filter about 180 L of plasma a day. Plasma sodium is about 140 mmol/L, so roughly

$$180 \text{ L/day} \times 140 \text{ mmol/L} \approx 25{,}000 \text{ mmol/day}$$

of sodium is filtered. Daily intake, and therefore daily excretion in a steady state, is around 100–200 mmol. So the kidney reabsorbs more than 99% of what it filters. A tiny change in the fraction reabsorbed is a large change in what is excreted, which is why small drug effects in the nephron can matter so much clinically.

## Segment by segment

| Segment | Share of filtered Na⁺ reabsorbed | Main apical transporter | Drug that acts here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proximal tubule | ~65% | Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger (NHE3), Na⁺-glucose cotransporters | Acetazolamide (indirectly), SGLT2 inhibitors |
| Thick ascending limb | ~25% | Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻ cotransporter (NKCC2) | Loop diuretics (furosemide) |
| Distal convoluted tubule | ~5% | Na⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter (NCC) | Thiazides |
| Collecting duct (principal cells) | ~2–3% | Epithelial Na⁺ channel (ENaC) | Amiloride; spironolactone acts upstream via aldosterone |

In every segment the energy comes from the same place: the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase on the *basolateral* membrane keeps intracellular sodium low, so sodium flows into the cell down its gradient through whatever apical route that segment has. Learning the apical transporter for each segment is most of the work.

### Proximal tubule

The workhorse. Reabsorption here is *isosmotic*: water follows sodium almost exactly, so the fluid leaving the proximal tubule has the same osmolality as plasma. Sodium enters mainly in exchange for H⁺, which is also how bicarbonate is reclaimed, and alongside glucose and amino acids.

SGLT2 inhibitors block sodium–glucose cotransport here. They lower blood glucose by making the kidney excrete it, and they cause a mild natriuresis. The renal and cardiac protection they give is now a bigger reason to prescribe them than glucose control, though the mechanism is still argued over.

### Thick ascending limb

Impermeable to water. It pumps salt out without water following, which dilutes the tubular fluid and builds the concentration gradient in the medulla that the collecting duct later uses to concentrate urine.

NKCC2 carries one Na⁺, one K⁺ and two Cl⁻ into the cell. Some of the K⁺ leaks back into the lumen through ROMK channels, which leaves the lumen positively charged, and that positive charge drives Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ through the gaps between cells. That is why loop diuretics cause calcium and magnesium loss as well as sodium loss.

### Distal convoluted tubule

NCC, the thiazide target. Thiazides *reduce* calcium excretion, the opposite of loop diuretics. That is useful in patients with calcium kidney stones, and a classic exam contrast.

### Collecting duct

The fine-tuning segment, and the one under hormonal control. Aldosterone increases the number of open ENaC channels and the activity of the basolateral pump. Sodium entering through ENaC makes the lumen negative, which drives K⁺ secretion through ROMK and H⁺ secretion from intercalated cells.

This one mechanism explains a lot:

- Anything that delivers more sodium to the collecting duct (loop diuretics, thiazides) increases K⁺ loss → hypokalaemia.
- Blocking ENaC (amiloride) or aldosterone (spironolactone) reduces K⁺ loss → these are "potassium-sparing", and can cause hyperkalaemia, especially with ACE inhibitors.

## What changes sodium reabsorption

- **Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system.** Low perfusion pressure → renin → angiotensin II (which increases proximal reabsorption directly) → aldosterone (collecting duct).
- **Natriuretic peptides.** Released when the heart is stretched; they increase filtration and reduce collecting duct reabsorption.
- **Sympathetic activity.** Increases renin release and proximal reabsorption.
- **Glomerulotubular balance.** If GFR rises, the proximal tubule reabsorbs proportionally more, so a change in filtration doesn't flood the distal nephron.

## Questions the group got wrong

1. *Why does furosemide cause hypokalaemia when its target transporter carries potassium into the cell?* Because the bigger effect is downstream: more sodium reaches the collecting duct, more is reabsorbed through ENaC, and more potassium is secreted in exchange.
2. *Which diuretic would you avoid in a patient with recurrent calcium stones?* A loop diuretic; a thiazide would actually help.
3. *A patient on spironolactone and ramipril has a K⁺ of 6.4.* Both reduce aldosterone's effect, one by blocking its receptor and one by lowering its production. Stop the spironolactone and treat the hyperkalaemia.
