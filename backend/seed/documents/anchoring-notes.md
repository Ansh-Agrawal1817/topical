# Anchoring and Adjustment (ECON 214 revision)

My revision notes for the behavioural block. Mostly from lectures 6–8 plus the Tversky & Kahneman reading. Sharing in case they help anyone else in the module; corrections welcome, especially on the policy section, which I'm least sure about.

## The basic idea

When people estimate a number, they often start from a value they've just seen (the **anchor**) and adjust away from it, but **not far enough**. So the final estimate is pulled towards the anchor, even when the anchor is obviously irrelevant.

The classic demonstration (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974): participants spun a wheel of fortune rigged to stop at either 10 or 65, then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. People who got 10 gave a median estimate of 25%; people who got 65 gave 45%. The wheel was *visibly random* and it still moved the answers by twenty points.

Key point from lecture: this isn't people being stupid. It's a reasonable strategy (start somewhere, adjust) that goes wrong because adjustment is effortful and people stop once they reach a value that seems plausible.

## Two explanations (lecture 7)

| Account | Mechanism | Prediction |
|---|---|---|
| Insufficient adjustment | Start at anchor, adjust until plausible, stop too early | Bigger effect when people are tired, distracted or rushed |
| Selective accessibility | The anchor makes anchor-consistent information come to mind first | Bigger effect when the anchor is a plausible value |

The lecturer's view was that both happen. Self-generated anchors (where you come up with the starting point yourself) fit insufficient adjustment better; anchors that someone gives you fit selective accessibility better. *(I think this distinction is likely to come up — it was in last year's paper too.)*

## Evidence it matters outside the lab

- **Pricing.** "Was £120, now £60" works partly because £120 is an anchor. Same with the expensive item placed first on a menu, which makes the second most expensive one look reasonable.
- **Negotiation.** Whoever makes the first offer sets the anchor. Studies of negotiation find the first offer is a strong predictor of the final price. Counter-intuitive takeaway: an aggressive first offer often helps, *if* you can justify it.
- **Courts.** Englich, Mussweiler and Strack had experienced German judges read a case file and then roll dice that were loaded to give a high or low number before suggesting a sentence. The judges who rolled high gave longer sentences. Lecturer called this "the slide that ruins the module for law students".
- **Credit card minimum payments.** Showing a minimum payment on a statement anchors people's repayments downwards. This is one of the examples used in the UK debate about statement design.

## Where the evidence is weaker

Important for an essay: don't present anchoring as limitless.

- Effects are smaller when people have **expertise** and real incentives — but the judges study suggests expertise isn't full protection.
- Some replications of the *incidental* anchoring effects (anchors from completely unrelated tasks) have found small or null effects. The big replication projects found the classic anchoring effects replicate well, but the more surprising variants less so.
- Anchors seem to work best when people are **uncertain**. If you know the answer, an anchor does little.

## Policy and nudges (least confident about this part)

Anchoring is used deliberately in "choice architecture":

- **Suggested donation amounts** on charity forms. Higher suggestions raise average donations but can reduce the *number* of donors, so the net effect is not obvious.
- **Default contribution rates** in pensions are partly anchoring and partly inertia; hard to separate. Lecturer said not to call defaults pure anchoring in the exam.

Ethical question from the seminar: if a firm uses anchors to raise prices it's manipulation, but if a government uses them to raise pension savings it's a nudge — is that consistent? My view is that the difference is whose interest it serves and whether people would endorse it if they knew, but I'm not sure that's the "right" answer for the essay.

## Exam checklist

- Define anchoring and adjustment in one sentence.
- Classic study + one real-world application with a *named* study.
- Both explanations, and which situations favour each.
- At least one limitation or replication point.
- If it's a policy question: separate anchoring from defaults/inertia.

Past paper question to practise: *"Anchoring shows that economic agents are not rational." Discuss.* (Careful — the good answers argue anchoring is consistent with bounded rationality rather than proving irrationality.)
